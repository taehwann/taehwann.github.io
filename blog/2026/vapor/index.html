Reflections on Vapor (Building a 3D Smoke Simulator)

Created on August 11, 2026

2026   ·   graphics   simulation   ·   computer-science

Vapor — A Real-Time 3D Smoke Simulator

I joined the Real-Time Graphics Lab at Hankuk University of Foreign Studies as a student researcher, and one of my assignments was to implement three papers on fluid simulation: Stable Fluids, An Unconditionally Stable MacCormack Method, and An Advection-Reflection Solver for Detail-Preserving Fluid Simulation.

That assignment eventually became Vapor, a real-time 3D smoke plume simulator built with C++20 and OpenGL volume ray-marching.

The goal was not simply to reproduce the equations from the papers. I wanted to understand what happens when those ideas have to become an actual working simulator.

What I Learned From the Papers

Each paper contributed something different to the simulator.

Stable Fluids gave me the foundation for understanding how semi-Lagrangian advection can be used to build a stable fluid solver.

I then implemented the MacCormack method to improve the accuracy of semi-Lagrangian advection to second order. Compared to the basic semi-Lagrangian approach, the difference was surprisingly visible: the smoke retained its structure and stayed energetic for much longer.

Finally, I implemented the main idea from the Advection-Reflection method to reduce the kinetic energy loss introduced by the projection step.

The implementation itself was not always as difficult as I expected. The staggered MAC grid initially looked intimidating, but once the data layout and indexing were clear, it became surprisingly intuitive. MacCormack was similar. Seeing the forward and backward advection steps side by side made the algorithm much easier to understand.

The difficult part was everything around those equations.

What Was Not Easy

I started with simple solid walls.

Then I thought an open top boundary would look much better. Instead of having smoke hit an invisible ceiling and bounce back down, I wanted it to naturally leave the simulation domain.

So I tried implementing open boundaries.

The smoke disappeared.

Not gradually. It just blew up and vanished from the screen.

I spent several days trying to figure out what was happening. Eventually, I found that the open boundary implementation introduced inconsistent pressure and velocity boundary conditions, allowing the projection step to produce nonphysical velocity behavior. The problem was not just a visual artifact. The boundary condition was repeatedly destabilizing the simulation, causing the velocity field to grow out of control.

I eventually gave up on the open boundary implementation and went back to solid no-slip walls.

And suddenly, everything worked.

The smoke rose smoothly, curled naturally, and finally looked like smoke.

It was a frustrating experience, but probably one of the most useful parts of the project. I learned that implementing a fluid simulation is not just about translating equations from a paper into code. There are many assumptions and interactions between different parts of the solver that only become apparent when the whole system is actually running.

CPU, GPU, and Debugging

One part I particularly enjoyed was implementing both CPU and GPU versions of the simulation.

The pressure solver and advection step can independently switch between CPU and GPU at runtime. I kept the GLSL compute shaders structurally close to their C++ counterparts, which made it possible to compare the two implementations frame by frame.

This was initially intended mainly for performance comparison, but it became extremely useful for debugging as well.

The GPU implementation could be fast, but when something went wrong, the CPU implementation gave me a much easier reference point. Being able to inspect intermediate values and compare the two paths made debugging much less of a guessing game.

There is something very satisfying about watching that work on the GPU.

What I Took Away From the Project

The biggest lesson was that good debugging data matters enormously.

When a simulation goes wrong, looking at the final rendered smoke is often not enough. The visualization tells you that something is wrong, but not necessarily where or why. Being able to inspect intermediate fields, compare CPU and GPU results, and track how values change over time made a huge difference.

I also learned a lot about working with AI while prototyping software.

AI can make it much faster to get a first implementation running, but the quality of the result depends heavily on how clearly the problem is described and what information is provided. I became much more aware of how to give an AI useful context, how to break a problem into smaller pieces, and how to use it as a prototyping and debugging tool rather than simply asking it to write the whole system.

Most importantly, I learned that reading a paper and implementing a paper are very different things.

A paper can explain the equations and the overall algorithm very clearly. But once you start implementing it, you have to make many additional decisions: how data is laid out, how boundaries are handled, how different stages interact, how to debug numerical instability, and how to verify that an implementation is actually doing what the paper describes.

Those are problems that are easy to overlook when reading the paper alone.

What Comes Next

For now, Vapor still uses a closed simulation domain, so eventually the smoke fills the box.

I may come back to open boundaries someday.

But next time, I know what I’m getting myself into.

For now, I’m just happy watching it work.
